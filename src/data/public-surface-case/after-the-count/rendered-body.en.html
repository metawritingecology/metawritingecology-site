After the Count

A relay baton in Yunlin, a name on a Taoyuan ballot, and what happens in Taipei after the votes are counted.

Case date28 November 2026

Source cutoff: 28 November 2026, 11:29 p.m. Taiwan time. Vote totals are from the Central Election Commission’s election-night count. The formal list of elected candidates had not yet been announced.

In August, Yunlin County Magistrate Chang Li-shan handed her niece, Chang Chia-chun, a relay baton and a list of public works projects at a press conference. Chang Chia-chun’s father had also served as county magistrate. The same surname had appeared on the door of the magistrate’s office and now appeared on campaign signs. The photograph showed who took the baton. The ballots would show who got the job.

On 28 November, all 638 polling stations in Yunlin reported their results. Liu Chien-kuo received 178,357 votes and Chang Chia-chun received 178,184. The difference was 173 votes. [1]

In Taoyuan’s first city council district, the name at the top of the count was sixteen characters long: 王妃天崩開局穿越整頓霸凌者佀廣洋, roughly, “A Princess With the Worst Possible Start Travels Through Time to Take On a Bully.” Wang kept her surname, which becomes part of the title “Princess” when read with the first character of her new given name. The full Chinese name also names her rival, 佀廣洋.

At the official candidates’ forum, she spoke about bullying and about how the prospect of a lawsuit could make people reluctant to speak. She came first with 29,533 votes. Her rival received 5,819 and did not win a seat. The count answered who would sit on the council. The allegation in the name still had to be judged on its evidence. [2]

In 1977, the photographer Chiu Wan-hsing went back to Guanyin, Taoyuan, to help his older brother watch the count. They wanted Hsu Hsin-liang to win. His brother brought a flashlight in case the power went out. If it did, someone who wanted another candidate to win would have needed that light too. [3]

Tonight in Taipei, Puma Shen (Shen Po-yang) lost. The incumbent mayor, Chiang Wan-an, received 781,312 votes. Shen’s 664,586 votes, or 45.69 percent, were the strongest showing by a DPP nominee for Taipei mayor since Chen Shui-bian’s 1998 reelection bid, both in votes and in vote share. Shen asked his supporters not to pass along claims that the count was rigged, and not to blame people who had not voted for him. “They are not our enemies. They are our neighbors.” He asked people to go back to the shows and books they had put aside. He also said he would pass their suggestions for the city on to Chiang. [4]

Some of the people listening had voted for the first time. Some had never before worked up the nerve to ask someone for a vote or explain to a stranger why they supported a candidate. “Shen Po-yang lost the election. You didn’t.” Those were things people had already done, and the result could not take them away. At Democratic Progressive Party headquarters, President Lai Ching-te spoke about a different responsibility: the central government would continue working with local governments regardless of which party had won. [5]

For people who had hoped to see a change in Taipei, New Taipei, Keelung, and Taoyuan, those 173 votes in Yunlin could not change the result in Taipei. Taipei’s result could not erase those 173 votes either. At the count, people who wanted different candidates to win could watch the same ballot being held up. The next day, they would still live in the same place.
