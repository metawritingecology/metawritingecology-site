When History Returns to the Street

French protests and public promises across Taiwan and Hong Kong

Case date18 October 2026
Original snapshot2026-10-18 · 04:01 UTC
Event record through2026-10-18 · 04:23 UTC

External public-discourse observation ≠ repository evidence. Date-bound research specimen; observation, structural reading and limits remain separate.

Event record through 18 October 2026, 04:23 UTC; 12:23 in Taiwan and Hong Kong; 06:23 CEST in metropolitan France. The French mobilisation took place on 17 octobre 2026 (#17octobre), local time.

The current French protests are read alongside selected movements in Taiwan and Hong Kong: how public claims take shape, how people sustain participation, and how policing, memory, and collective feeling enter the street.

The streets carry more than immediate demands. A song returns. A familiar monument acquires a new inscription. Someone who joined an earlier movement appears again; someone younger encounters that history through a melody, a flower, or a public square.

The comparison begins with two questions. What are people asking public institutions to deliver, and what enables them to remain in the street? It then follows another: how do earlier struggles become available to a new gathering?

The answer requires more than a scale of 民主成熟度, or democratic maturity. Political rights, social protection, organisational capacity, participation costs, and threats to a community’s future can develop unevenly. Historical resonance moves through these conditions. It does not remove their differences.

Movement 01

French demands converge

Observed surface

Before the 17 October mobilisation, Euronews described connections among gilets jaunes, or Yellow Vests, trade unions, student organisations, and other groups. Their demands included pouvoir d’achat, or purchasing power, education and health services, police violence, and public participation. The mobilisation brings several public disputes into a shared space. Euronews, 16 October

Reuters subsequently reported large peaceful marches alongside attacks on buildings, shops, and police by some groups. It traced recent student mobilisation to September grievances about teacher shortages and deteriorating school facilities. The conflict therefore includes both services publics, or public services, and the legitimacy of the response to people demanding them. Reuters, 17 October post-event report, carried by Internazionale

Local reporting also changes the scale of the picture. In Albi, La Dépêche described students and families marching over education, health, and young people’s prospects, with no major incident reported before dispersal around 18:00. The national mobilisation contained very different local experiences. La Dépêche, Albi, 17 October

Research reading

These grievances can connect through everyday experience: household costs rise while public provision fails to compensate; encounters with coercive enforcement then turn a dispute over services into a dispute over how the state treats those making demands.

The convergence is politically important precisely because the participants bring different priorities. Peaceful marches, confrontations, and destructive acts belong to the same event record, with responsibility attached to particular conduct.

Nearby European context

The Italian slogan IL FUTURO NON SI ARMA / SI INSEGNA, “The future is not armed; it is taught,” places education at the centre of a dispute over public priorities. It appeared in an ASES education-union statement on 7 September 2026. Spain’s housing marches on 3 October and Madrid’s public-school strike and 16 October march brought rent, staffing, class sizes, and pay into public space. These are parallel disputes with distinct organisations and political settings. ASES statement, EFE, housing protests, Europa Press, Madrid education protest

Movement 02

Welfare creates expectations as well as resources

Observed surface

The OECD’s 2026 survey of France identifies pressures from debt, interest costs, ageing, defence, and climate-related expenditure. It also considers how burdens are distributed between retirees and working-age people. Fiscal pressure makes the distribution of adjustment a political question. OECD Economic Surveys France 2026

Protection also has uneven effects. INSEE estimated that, in 2023, improvements in living standards covered roughly half the additional inflation-related expenditure of the poorest fifth of households, while exceeding the full additional cost for the richest fifth. INSEE analysis of 2023

Research reading

When public provision becomes part of how people organise their lives, its deterioration can be experienced as the retreat of a shared promise. The question becomes who pays, who waits, whose interests receive protection, and who participates in deciding the adjustment.

Existing welfare can help sustain participation and raise expectations simultaneously. It can also leave significant differences between generations, income groups, and places. “They already have benefits” therefore explains little about whether those benefits remain adequate, accessible, or fairly distributed.

Movement 03

Rights and the capacity to use them

Observed surface

In France’s private sector, the general droit de grève, or right to strike, can be exercised by employees who do not belong to a union, usually without advance notice. Public services and certain transport activities have different rules. Strike time can still be deducted from wages. French government guidance

Taiwan’s 勞資爭議處理法, the Act for Settlement of Labor-Management Disputes, generally requires unsuccessful mediation before industrial action under Article 53. Article 54 requires a direct, secret vote supported by more than half of all union members before a union declares a strike, and sets occupational restrictions and essential-service conditions. This statutory route operates through a union, unlike the general French private-sector route available to non-union employees. These are rules for labour disputes; street assemblies have a separate legal framework. Taiwan legislation

Research reading

The right to dissent and the practical capacity to miss several days of work are different things. Rent, care responsibilities, lost wages, injury, and legal costs remain consequential. Unions, mutual aid, legal support, household resources, and social protection can redistribute these burdens.

This helps refine the democratic-maturity hypothesis. Electoral competition and civil liberties may coexist with weak labour protection or unequal material security. Freedom House’s 2026 account describes Taiwan’s competitive democracy and peaceful transfers of power while also identifying labour and migrant-worker vulnerabilities. Freedom House, Taiwan 2026

Movement 04

Distribution and the future of the political community

Observed surface

Research on Taiwan’s 2014 太陽花運動, the Sunflower Movement, connects procedural justice and democratic governance with distrust of cross-strait policy. An economic agreement could become a question about who was entitled to decide the community’s future. Ting, Wei Ming, NTU thesis abstract, 2016

Taiwan’s Ministry of National Defense describes military, legal, and other coercive pressures from China. Ministry of National Defense public report

Hong Kong’s 2019 反修例運動, the anti-extradition-bill movement, expanded from the proposed legal change to demands including police accountability and universal suffrage. The future of rights and institutional autonomy became part of the dispute. Human Rights Watch retrospective

The later environment is materially different. Freedom House’s 2026 report describes restrictions following the 2020 National Security Law, the 2021 electoral changes, and the 2024 local national-security legislation. Reduced visible mobilisation cannot simply be read as reduced grievance. Freedom House, Hong Kong 2026

Research reading

Taiwan’s democratic self-government and military security, and Hong Kong’s autonomy and freedoms as a Chinese Special Administrative Region, are distinct institutional questions. The stakes and available institutional responses differ.

Threats to a community’s continuity may redirect attention, reshape distributional disputes, or encourage new solidarity. Anxiety can mobilise people as well as exhaust them. Its political effects depend on organisations, resources, and available channels of action.

The shared process is the conversion of privately borne difficulties into public claims. The available responses differ: negotiation, legislation, elections, or the struggle to preserve those avenues themselves.

Movement 05

Policing and the boundaries of action

Observed surface

Reuters reported on 17 October that France’s police watchdog was investigating at least 24 allegations of police violence. That investigation is part of the event, rather than an incidental qualification to accounts of protester violence. Reuters, carried by MarketScreener

In Hong Kong, Amnesty verified footage concerning 14 incidents of excessive police force on 12 June 2019. Its September statement separately called for investigation of whether undercover officers had incited violence. Excessive force, undercover deployment, and deliberate provocation are different claims, with different evidentiary requirements. Amnesty June investigation, Amnesty September statement

Taiwan’s Legislative Yuan occupation on 18 March 2014 should also be distinguished from the Executive Yuan events of 23–24 March. In a 2022 compensation judgment concerning the latter, the Taiwan High Court found a lawful basis for removal but held that some officers used disproportionate force. A legitimate objective did not make every method legitimate. Taiwan High Court judgment summary

Research reading

An account centred only on demonstrators losing control misses how policing affects the situation. At the same time, identifying a deliberate provocateur requires evidence of identity and conduct, and any alleged direction requires evidence of its own. Police exercise public power and carry corresponding duties of lawful, proportionate force and accountability.

Movements also negotiate their own boundaries. A 不要衝! sign, “do not charge,” appeared outside Taiwan’s legislature on 24 May 2024 during 青鳥行動, the Bluebird Movement. Rti photograph and commentary

The wording 慎防內賊／不要被煽動／不要衝國會 asks people to guard against betrayal from within, resist incitement, and avoid storming parliament. These appeals try to keep escalation from determining the movement’s course.

The protective rationale is explicit in 農藝女孩看世界’s statements of 22 and 23 May 2024: conserve the movement’s energy, avoid bloodshed, and remember the 太陽花 experience. Holding back can preserve people and the capacity to mobilise again. The warning about 內賊 expresses a fear of provocation; identifying an infiltrator remains a separate evidentiary question. The 2025 大罷免, or mass-recall mobilisation, developed another route for political pressure through formal petitions and voting. 22 May statement, 23 May statement, Central Election Commission notice

Coordinating emergency access

Hong Kong demonstrators made room for an ambulance on 16 June 2019. Reuters video, carried by The Washington Post

Au Poste’s account of Paris on 17 October 2026 describes barricades opening for firefighters, then closing after the vehicle passed. Occupying a street can include reorganising it for emergency access. Such coordination makes care part of the occupation itself: a route can be contested and still be kept available for emergency passage. Au Poste field account

La Dépêche supplies a second, local French example: people blocking Albi’s Pont Neuf let an emergency fire-service vehicle through, with applause. The passage temporarily reorganised the priorities of the blockade. La Dépêche field report

Care through professional roles

Care also travels through professional roles. HK01 reported that, on the Tokyo–Hong Kong flight CX505 on 26 July 2019, a captain explained the peaceful airport protest and wished Hongkongers encouragement and safety. An everyday professional address became a gesture of concern across the boundary of the demonstration. HK01, published 27 July 2019

Creating prospective witnesses

Advance statements of personal intent can perform another kind of public care: creating prospective witnesses. They ask others to remember what a person has declared, preserve the record, and ask questions if harm later occurs. Their purpose can be to prevent that harm from being prematurely dismissed, explained away, or obscured. The declaration prepares public memory in advance. It asks an audience to remain answerable to a person’s stated intention, even when that person may later be unable to speak.

Movement 06

The Hôtel de Ville de Limoges

Observed surface

The Hôtel de Ville de Limoges was built between 1879 and 1883, enabled by Alfred Fournier’s bequest and designed by Charles-Alfred Leclerc. It opened on 14 July 1883, making it 143 years old in 2026. Its history belongs to the Third Republic. Its façades and roofs, excluding the rear addition, and the square’s fountain received heritage protection in 1975. French Ministry of Culture record

AFP reported the fire on 17 October. The prefect said people broke windows, entered, and set it alight. The prefect and mayor described much of the building as lost, with the roof and clock tower collapsed. The reported destruction was extensive; the identities and individual motives of those responsible remain unresolved. AFP, carried by TV5MONDE

Research reading

A town hall makes public authority visible. It is also a shared facility and an inherited civic asset. Its destruction can therefore damage the very community whose claims are being articulated in the street.

The fire brings authority and common property into the same image. A building that embodies the state also holds services, memories, and resources used by the public. Its loss makes the tension materially consequential.

Movement 07

Memory returns through people and music

Observed surface

The French Republic institutionalises revolutionary memory. The Élysée’s historical account explains how the national holiday established in 1880 connected 14 July 1789 with the Fête de la Fédération of 1790: popular revolt and civic unity coexist within the commemoration. The Limoges town hall’s opening date places it within that republican calendar. Élysée history of 14 July

France also has living movement lineages, visible in the return of gilets jaunes to the current coalition. Taiwan’s recent movements provide specific records of connections among living participants and organisations. A 2025 青鳥 anniversary event explicitly connected earlier campus struggles, 野百合學運, or the Wild Lily Movement, 太陽花, 青鳥, and 大罷免. An NCCU study likewise examines prior participation, emotion, and information networks in the later mobilisation. Participants and organisations actively make these connections across generations. Anniversary event description, Lin I-Ying, NCCU thesis abstract, 2025

Music makes some of these connections audible. CNA records people inside and outside Taiwan’s legislature singing the 太陽花 song 〈島嶼天光〉, “Island’s Sunrise,” on 21 May 2024. A photographed ensemble at the 28 May 青鳥 gathering played 〈望春風〉, “Longing for the Spring Breeze,” 〈島嶼天光〉, and 〈晚安台灣〉, “Good Night, Taiwan.” CNA, Newtalk field report

In Hong Kong, musicologist Ho Chak Law traces the use of a Cantonese adaptation of “Do You Hear the People Sing?” during the 2014 雨傘運動, or Umbrella Movement, and English and Cantonese performances during 2019 gatherings. A melody can reconnect earlier experience while addressing a new audience. Smithsonian Folklife

Hong Kong also produced new music. 〈願榮光歸香港〉, “Glory to Hong Kong,” emerged during the 2019 movement. Bryan K. M. Mok’s autoethnographic study records September 商場合唱, collective singing in shopping malls, including gatherings known as 和你唱, “sing with you.” His account examines how singing could turn a commercial interior into a place of collective expression. This new composition and the reused melody from Les Misérables have different histories, even when both help sustain a gathering. Mok, Religions, 2021

The current French material includes another repertoire. Watson reports the Orchestre du Nouveau Monde playing music from Avengers and The Hanging Tree at Paris’s Place de la République on 17 October, and later being exposed to tear gas. The orchestra’s own manifesto connects music with public action, peaceful civil disobedience, and social and climate justice. Watson, Orchestre du Nouveau Monde manifesto

In its 17 October communiqué, the Orchestre du Nouveau Monde states that it gathered in solidarity with lycée mobilisation. Its rehearsal began at 11:30; the orchestra says it was playing Avengers at about 12:20 when police tear-gas and stun grenades were directed towards the musicians. It describes playing for nearly twenty more minutes before dispersing, leaving the planned 13:00 concert unable to proceed. Télérama’s interview with volunteer and press contact Étienne Cognet also recounts the dispersal. Here music is both a way to sustain a gathering and an activity exposed to policing. Télérama, 17 October

Research reading

Continuity takes several forms: returning participants, reused organising experience, and new participants inheriting stories. Music brings them into contact.

Performance gives that continuity a bodily form. People wait, breathe, keep time, and produce sound together. Familiar music can carry courage, grief, humour, or the feeling of remaining present into another gathering. A shared rhythm allows different experiences to occupy the same duration.

Historical distance remains important. The barricade events in Les Misérables, or 《悲慘世界》, concern the June uprising of 1832. That later uprising belongs to the layered history through which revolutionary imagery travels. Groupe Hugo research on June 1832

Experience crosses borders

Radio Parleur’s Paris field report from 18 August 2019 records gilets jaunes displaying umbrellas in solidarity with Hong Kong and describing what they had watched and hoped to learn through social media. Radio Parleur, 2019

Le HuffPost reported French protesters borrowing Hong Kong’s cones-and-umbrellas repertoire in November 2019. Le HuffPost, 16 November 2019

These exchanges give protest memory an international dimension. Familiar forms travel through reporting, social networks, and direct observation, then acquire meaning within another place’s struggles. A specific chain of learning should be traced rather than inferred from resemblance alone.

Movement 08

Public symbols and circulating images

Observed surface

Marianne personifies the French Republic. Watson’s 17 October account records the statue at Place de la République being dressed in a gilet jaune carrying an accusation about police injuries to school students. A republican symbol becomes a surface for questioning republican enforcement. The monument becomes a way to address the Republic in its own symbolic language. Élysée on Marianne, Watson field reporting

Au Poste’s Paris account also records a student dressed as Napoléon. The costume makes a historical figure available for play, quotation, and circulation. The encounter between costume and present-day grievance gives the street a theatrical dimension. Au Poste

Public imagery and competing interpretations

Circulating imagery brings a cheval de Troie, or Trojan-horse-style prop, and a Batman costume into the repertoire. Alongside Marianne and Napoléon, they place classical history, national memory, and popular fiction within reach of the crowd. An orchestral image’s phrase “Avengers Assemble” pairs a familiar call to gather with convergence des luttes, the convergence of struggles. Recognition becomes an invitation to participate.

The barricade carries a different historical charge. An online juxtaposition describes a contemporary block barrier and an old barricade as almost the same scene 155 years apart, invoking 1871 and the Paris Commune. The gesture makes a claim of continuity: the present is made legible through an earlier struggle. That claim is itself part of the political work of the image.

Familiarity can organise solidarity, humour, or menace. Invocations of Louis XVI give revolutionary memory a threatening register; costumes allow play within confrontation. A call for restraint can also be recirculated as a criticism of restraint. The public meaning of an image continues to change as people caption, quote, and argue over it.

Research reading

Bodies occupying a monument, musicians gathering in a square, and people opening an emergency passage all make claims about how public space should be used. These acts work through different relations to authority: accusation, collective expression, protection, and interruption.

The same gathering can appear heroic, comic, caring, threatening, or chaotic. The important question is how a particular form invites participation and directs attention, and what responsibilities accompany it.

Alongside 鍘美藝術節

The project’s own earlier dated readings of 鍘美藝術節, the Zha Mei arts festival, followed a public controversy into familiar theatrical roles and an existing repertoire of glove puppetry, Taiwanese opera, ritual, and popular song. The September reading emphasised selection and recombination: recognisable works already carried themes that a new occasion could activate. 18 August specimen, 19 September follow-up

Cosplay, film music, and the cheval de Troie offer a bounded comparison. They can lower the effort of recognising a scene and provide a way to join it through material already charged with feeling. Familiar forms carry an affective history before the new occasion begins.

The purposes and exposure remain different. A festival organises participation through a performance setting; a protest also confronts public decisions, street control, and potentially coercive enforcement. Familiarity can help make both settings accessible, while carrying very different costs and responsibilities. The comparison concerns this shared use of recognisable cultural material within different political situations.

Movement 09

Visible streets and distributed costs

Research reading

A limited comparison with MWE’s Cost Visibility and Redistribution directs attention to which burdens become publicly actionable. Disruption and damage are immediately visible. Waiting, missing services, unpaid care, and the work of compensating for institutional failure are often distributed among households.

Protest can be read as an attempt to bring those dispersed burdens into a shared field of decision. Visibility alone does not establish that costs have been fairly redistributed.

False Legibility offers a second limit. A familiar crowd, song, costume, or revolutionary image can make a distant event feel understood before its institutional conditions have been examined. The difficulty arises when recognition of the form substitutes for that inquiry. Recognition becomes a starting point for inquiry, with the institutional differences kept in view.

The MWE terms are used here as case-specific interpretive prompts.

Public Surface Case

What this snapshot preserves

The French protests make a tension between public promises and lived conditions visible. Taiwan and Hong Kong show how distributional claims can coexist with efforts to preserve the institutions through which claims can be made.

History returns through selective reuse: a building, a melody, a flower, a remembered gathering, an improvised passage for emergency care. The question is what each return asks the present to recognise, and which differences remain beneath the resonance.

Public Surface Case

French reporting and maps

The following routes provide national context, local reporting, and different kinds of geographical information.

  • LCP’s 16 October explainer sets out the announced coalition and grievances, including teacher shortages, school buildings, class sizes, and Parcoursup. Published before the event, it establishes calls and intentions rather than turnout.
  • La Dépêche’s Albi field report provides a geographically specific account. It helps retain local variation within a national story.
  • Linternaute’s 17 October live synthesis brings together reporting and attributed statements from several outlets and officials. It explicitly distinguishes mapped calls to mobilise from gatherings actually observed. Its figures retain the attribution and timing of those sources.
  • Ouest-France’s reported-gatherings map is listed by the publisher as a map of cities where gatherings took place on 17 October. The listing was accessible for this snapshot; the map’s entries and methodology could not be independently inspected.
  • The organisers’ Carte des mobilisations mixes announced actions, visitor-added locations, and infrastructure such as stations and roundabouts. Its own data notes describe several inputs. Its markers therefore include calls and logistical locations, not only observed gatherings.
Public Surface Case

Sources and scope

This is a dated comparative reading. News reports establish attributed events; legislation and statistics provide institutional background; the interpretive sections examine the social functions of memory, performance, care, and public claims. Historical statistics and selected studies are not a comparative survey of today’s participants.

The orchestra’s communiqué and Télérama’s interview partly share a participant-source chain. The communiqué supplies the detailed rehearsal chronology; only the publicly accessible part of the interview was read. Déclaré means notified or declared, rather than formally authorised. Its account of police action remains attributed testimony.

The longer Taiwanese restraint slogan is read as a public appeal, while the linked May statements establish an authored protective rationale. The position of an unidentified reposter is not assigned. Visual examples with incomplete original-post provenance are used only for symbolic and rhetorical analysis, not to establish event chronology or responsibility for violence. Personal images and declarations, including material that might identify minors, are not reproduced.

The 2019 cross-border reports document exchange at that time; the learning history of particular 2026 actions remains open. This account keeps France, Taiwan, and Hong Kong in their distinct institutional settings and does not treat similar imagery as a common national condition or a single coordinated movement.

Subsequent event counts, investigations, and outcomes belong to a later dated reading.
