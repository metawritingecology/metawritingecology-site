Elections / Public Space / Administrative Neutrality

Neutrality Is Not the Middle

A campaign rally, a metro entrance, and the rules that decide who can use public space in Taipei.

Case date26 November 2026
Source cutoff26 November 2026

01

What the middle measures

Two days before Taipei’s election-eve rallies, the city had announced two sets of bus diversions near City Hall: one for a campaign rally and another for an election-night gathering. Taiwan’s local elections were set for November 28, 2026, and voters in the capital would choose a mayor. A campaign could apply to hold a rally on a road. Changes to bus routes were the city’s responsibility. [1, 2, 3]

Among the candidates were the incumbent mayor, Chiang Wan-an of the Kuomintang (KMT), and the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) candidate, Puma Shen (Shen Po-yang), a sitting legislator. [4] A survey commissioned by the Xinmin Association asked separately whether each man was suited to be mayor. The survey report grouped responses into nine categories. Its three middle groups included respondents who rated both candidates equally positively (G4), gave no clear answer on either candidate (G5), or rated both equally negatively (G6). [5]

G6 describes a pair of answers, not a political identity. The nine groups are not intervals on a bell curve. Equal ratings on suitability alone tell us neither what someone thinks of the parties nor whether they intend to vote. And the rules that shape a street, a station or a public room apply to people whether they take part in the election or not.

02

What a booking shows

Chiang’s campaign confirmed applications for two road segments for its November 27 rally: Ketagalan Boulevard, in front of the Presidential Office, and adjoining Zhongshan South Road. It had also booked Shifu Road, beside Taipei City Hall, on November 27 to prepare for a separate event on election night, November 28. The bookings were for separate events. [6, 7]

By November 25, the city’s Public Transportation Office had issued a separate notice for the planned election-night gathering: from 10 p.m. on November 27 until 4 a.m. on November 29, 15 bus routes were scheduled to skip four stops, including the City Hall stop. That notice was already public when Chiang’s campaign offered the road to Shen. The notice was for the count-night gathering. It did not say what would happen to the buses if a different rally took its place. [3]

On November 25 at 9:30 a.m., the first phase of traffic restrictions began around the Ren’ai Road site of Shen’s planned November 27 rally. The police plan restricted the bus lanes, inner lanes and the north-side slow lanes between Yixian Road and Guangfu South Road; the south-side slow lanes were reserved for residents. The city’s advance notice listed detours affecting 14 bus routes and nine stops. The city’s broader, four-phase police plan spanned several dates and covered both planned gatherings near City Hall. It does not describe a single continuous closure or four days of stage construction. [8, 2, 9]

That evening, Shen announced he was cancelling the in-person rally on Ren’ai Road. He said he and the city had not found a traffic arrangement that worked. His team dismantled the stage overnight, and reporters found traffic moving again around Ren’ai and Yixian roads the next morning. [9, 10]

In the early hours of November 26, Chiang’s campaign manager, Luo Chih-chiang, said the incumbent was willing to give up the November 27 Shifu Road booking so Shen’s campaign could use it. Chiang confirmed the offer later that morning. Shen said there was not enough time to rehearse or make the necessary safety arrangements, including keeping access clear for medical emergencies. The sources reviewed for this case do not show a completed transfer. [7, 11]

Before choosing Ren’ai Road, Shen’s campaign had considered holding its rally on Beiping East Road, outside its headquarters. It had booked November 27 there, but not the preceding two days. Shen said his team needed two to three days for staging and rehearsal and had to leave room for emergency access. The two campaigns described different timetables. Luo said Chiang’s team planned to put up its Ketagalan Boulevard stage on the day of the rally. The police plan for that event nevertheless called for closures on Ketagalan Boulevard from 10 p.m. the previous evening. The time to erect a stage, the time needed on site to prepare an event, and the period during which traffic is restricted are different measures. Neither campaign’s estimate establishes a general minimum. [11, 12, 13, 14]

Under Taipei’s rules, the order of receipt matters when applications overlap in place and time. Screenshots showed submissions apparently arriving within seconds of the system opening for the two earlier dates on Beiping East Road. The city’s public works office said the applicants were private individuals and did not identify them with either campaign. A timestamp is not a road-use approval or a police record for an assembly. Nor does it show whether a person or a script submitted the form, or whether the road was ultimately used. [15, 16]

A different kind of booking dispute arose in March 2025, while recall campaigners were gathering signatures to bring sitting legislators to a recall vote. In Taipei, a city councillor drew attention to hundreds of reservations made by the office of KMT legislator Hsu Chiao-hsin. The count covered time slots at twelve neighbourhood community centres, not hundreds of separate venues. Hsu’s office maintained that it had activities planned and other slots were still available. A booking count cannot tell us how many of those slots were used, cancelled or unavailable to other applicants who sought the same time. [17, 18]

03

Where the rule applies

Taipei Metro said its staff asked people distributing literature at Nanjing Fuxing station to move outside the area it manages because they were affecting pedestrian flow. At Daan station, staff put up queue barriers while the incumbent’s campaign handed out stickers. The operator said the handout took place outside its managed area, and that it had also used barriers for an event near Dahu Park station. The response differed; so, on the operator’s account, did the location and circumstances. A comparison would need the exact boundary and records of similar activity under the same conditions. [19]

A different question arises inside a city department. In November 2018, Taipei’s then health commissioner, Huang Shih-chieh, shared a link to the re-election website of the then mayor, Ko Wen-je, in a department LINE group used for work. Huang said the material concerned health policy and was shared for staff information. The report documents the post and his explanation; it does not establish a finding of misconduct. A link sent by a friend and one posted by a supervisor in an official group can carry different weight. Taiwan’s administrative neutrality law distinguishes personal political activity from conduct that draws on official authority or public resources. [20, 21]

04

Different demands in the same street

An earlier piece in this series followed how demands about purchasing power, education and health services converged around France’s October 17 mobilisation. The groups involved included Yellow Vest participants, trade unions and student organisations. Sharing public space did not make them a single political bloc. Taipei poses a different question: what should the people who manage public space owe to groups whose aims differ? [22]

In Taipei, no one expects rival campaigns to be impartial about who wins. The people administering the roads and stations had a different job. After a rally is cancelled and a road reopens, the record can still show who applied, when the city acted and which restrictions took effect. Those are the records needed to compare how two similar requests were handled.
