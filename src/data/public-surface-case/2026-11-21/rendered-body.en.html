City / Performance / Circulation

The City Between Shows

Two stages, three stops, and the streets a concert leaves behind.

Case date21 November 2026
Source cutoff21 November 2026

01

Three stops

At five in the afternoon, Julia Peng is scheduled to sing at Kaohsiung Arena. At seven, BTS are due onstage at the National Stadium. The stations that serve the two venues are three stops apart. A concert ticket tells you which way to go. The same line carries both audiences. On the platform, no one has to agree on which concert the city is holding.[1]

For fans in the south, a concert ticket used to come with another decision: how to get north, and how to get back. A southern fan interviewed by READr remembered the high-speed rail fare as a substantial part of the cost of seeing a show. After years of taking that trip for granted, the complaint can now come from Taipei: why is it always Kaohsiung? It is not that the trip south is any less tiring. It is that when Kaohsiung fans made the same trip north, the journey rarely seemed like a problem anyone else had to notice.[12]

There are earlier evenings on the same line. Bruno Mars and Energy played the two venues on 7 September 2024. On 22 November 2025, TWICE played the stadium and Wu Bai played the Arena; Sun Shu-mei and the National Taiwan Symphony Orchestra were performing elsewhere in the city. Kaohsiung Metro recorded 349,931 trips that day. That figure counts journeys across a system serving more than the concerts, not people admitted to either show. It does, however, tell us something that the ticket counts cannot: a concert has a second venue, the transport network that fills and empties the first.[2]

Outside World Games Station, the way home was posted at street level. In one spectator’s footage, a large roadside screen marks the spot with “You Are Here.” Another spectator remembers staff explaining how to leave after the show.[18]

For TWICE, the night was the group's first concert in Taiwan, ten years after its debut. For Tzuyu it could also be called coming home. The group recorded the metro's arrival announcements. The train approached the station in their voices.[3]

02

Where the stage was built

The train fare is the part of a much larger imbalance a listener can see on a receipt. Public construction and investment, industry and work were unevenly distributed. Long before Kaohsiung was counting the crowds at international tour stops, Taiwan’s planning and budget records were already describing a lopsided geography: functions concentrated in Taipei, and public investment that had not closed regional gaps. The south was not without industry or culture. But having musicians, having the money to keep a venue open and having the power to decide where a venue gets built are different things.[13]

The Kaohsiung Music Center makes that difference concrete. The city had to make a case for a music centre at the harbour. The central plan changed sites and added a marine-culture remit before approval in 2009; the construction budget was later increased. A survey of the local music scene records the everyday version of the same problem: musicians looking north for producers, recording facilities and rehearsal space. Fire EX. formed among schoolmates in Kaohsiung, but eventually based itself in Taipei to work on music full-time.[14]

Megaport did not have to wait for the new building. It started at the harbour in 2006, with a team that had organised Taipei’s Formoz Festival. So the story is not that the north had nothing to contribute. It is that people in Kaohsiung acquired more room to decide what would be heard. In 2015, that meant making space for Hong Kong singer Denise Ho to perform, and for Taiwan’s Lin Fei-fan and Hong Kong’s Joshua Wong to talk about the movements they had been part of. These were choices made by people running a particular festival, not a political position held by everyone in the crowd.[4][15]

Fire EX. could make Taiwan audible in songs such as Island’s Sunrise and work with Takeshi Hosomi and Masafumi Isobe on a Japanese release. Another Kaohsiung band, Elephant Gym, took a different route: out of a collaboration with Japan’s toe came a song that worked a piece of toe’s Two Moons into its own music. Those are exchanges in which something changed on the recording, not just occasions on which an overseas name appeared on a poster.[16]

At a campaign rally in Taichung’s Wuri District on 20 November, Fire EX. sang Twin City (〈雙城記〉), the song it made with Hong Kong lyricist Lin Xi in 2019 in solidarity with Hong Kong’s anti-extradition movement. It had opened a Hong Kong solidarity concert in Taipei that year. When the band sang it at Taipei Arena in July 2026, people in the audience held up signs in support of Hong Kong booksellers. In Wuri, a photo from the crowd shows the Cantonese words 「掛住你，望早歸」 behind the band: we miss you; we hope you come home soon. The rally was for a local election, but the song was also speaking to Hong Kong. The freedom it asked for was not Taiwan’s alone.[17]

03

A crossing for the audience

The first Megaport Festival invited Hsieh Chin-yen to perform at the harbour. She came back for its twentieth anniversary; on the same year's programme were milet and a collaboration between Li Chu-hsin and Nymphia Wind. In between, the festival put Chthonic and Collage together on a stage. The place was already doing more than waiting for an international tour to put it on a map. Its organisers could decide what would be heard together.[4]

In 2017, the organisers had a less glamorous decision to make: audiences needed to get from the stages at Pier-2 to those across the water in Penglai. A temporary floating bridge gave them a way across. The permanent rotating Great Harbor Bridge opened in 2020. It is a different structure. This November, the permanent bridge is one of the places lit for BTS.[5]

Put the two crossings beside each other. The first changes where an audience can go. The other, in its role as a purple landmark, changes what people can see of a concert they may not be attending. A city can make a show visible to people who do not have a ticket. It still has to get the people who do have one home.

On 1 April 2023, Mayday at the stadium, A-Mei at the Arena and Megaport at the harbour shared a Saturday without sharing a stage. The metro counted 252,503 trips that day. Along the waterfront that week, the lights took turns: Mayday blue, Megaport orange, A-Mei purple and rainbow. The railway had to carry all three audiences at once. The lights could address them in turn.[6]

04

The street joins in

Making room for music made here did not rule out making room for a tour made elsewhere. But the welcome still had to be made locally. The tour is called ARIRANG. Kaohsiung calls its welcome BEST TO SHARE. There is a red installation by the stadium and there are purple lights out on the streets. The touring production placed an installation at the venue. The municipality lit a park. The shops nearby put up lights of their own. Seen from the street, the notices separating those projects were less visible than the lights.[7]

Central Park was lit for the visit. Yuzhu and Nanhua put up purple lights of their own. At Ruifeng Night Market, dancers performed Butter; one viewer online recognised the Los Angeles crosswalk where BTS had performed the song in 2021. That resemblance is part of how the scene travelled, not evidence that the organisers explicitly set out to recreate it.[8]

Kaohsiung Tong put photographs of stations, towers, a night market and hotels into a single grid. One English-language repost read the grid as Taiwan pulling out all the stops. Shops and public agencies had done different things in different places. In the grid they shared a colour; in the repost they acquired one subject. This is how a series of local decisions comes to look, from elsewhere, like a country acting in unison.

05

The camera turns around

Before they were lit purple, the Dragon and Tiger Pagodas had already appeared in another account of Kaohsiung. Fujii Kaze brought a breakfast shop, a park karaoke session and the pagodas onto the stadium screen during Seishun Sick. Some listeners later went looking for the seat they had seen him take. A concert had given them somewhere to go the next day.[9]

On the BTS rest day, Jung Kook filmed another Kaohsiung. He posted it as his free time, with Taiwanese guitarist Dong Yun-chang's Blue Sea and Sky underneath. The film is not the footage shot by bystanders at Liuhe Night Market two days earlier. In one, the artist decides what to put in the frame and what to make public. In the other, he is the person being filmed.[10]

06

Another city in purple

In October, before the first concert in Lima, Jung Kook had posted photographs of that city as well: streets, traffic and the wetlands at Pantanos de Villa. In Surco, the arch at the Friendship Park was lit purple and a nearby street briefly carried the name of BTS. These are not rehearsals for Kaohsiung. Put them alongside it and purple no longer proves that one city welcomed the group in a way no other city could.[10][11]

When the Liuhe footage circulated, some viewers praised the room the members seemed to have. Some Latin American fans asked whether close filming and people calling out the members’ names would have been read as kindly had the clip come from their city. A walk through a night market is not the same as a crowd keeping people awake outside a hotel. Nor does that difference explain why the hotel became the scene through which some people remember Lima, or why a short clip should stand in for how an entire city behaves.[11]

On a hiking trail in Taiwan, a visitor from Mallorca remembered strangers stopping just to welcome him. A woman who served him and his companions homemade cola asked if she could take a photo with them.[19] The greeting and the request left room for an answer. So does the small pause when someone looks lost: you can slow down to give them a chance to ask and keep walking if they don’t.

Before a later Lima show, supporters in the stadium began chanting: don't go to the hotel. They did not have to stop being fans to draw that line. A city can turn on the lights and a crowd can welcome someone. Neither is entitled to decide where that person's time off ends.[11]
