By the time we reach Goose Town, it has already been collecting taxes for sixteen years. The accounts are less forthcoming about where those years begin.

An incoming official approaches by train. Horses pull it along the rails. Inside the carriage, the government is travelling comfortably. Outside, the horses are doing the work.[8]

There will be a change of administration before arrival.

01Before the government

At Xiaozhan, south of Tianjin, soldiers learned new formations. Their uniforms, weapons, training, and officers belonged to a late Qing effort to reorganise military power. In 1895, Yuan Shikai took charge of the Newly Created Army, or Xinjian Lujun. Among the officers associated with this establishment were men whose names appear in accounts of presidents, cabinets, and civil wars.[1]

The name Beiyang already belonged to another part of that effort. Under Li Hongzhang, the Beiyang Fleet had been organised for the defence of northern waters. Its ships and coastal responsibilities were not the same institution as Yuan’s army.[2]

The Beiyang Army was not a fleet that came ashore.

After Yuan succeeded Li as governor-general of Zhili and Beiyang minister in 1901, his military establishment expanded. The Beiyang divisions became a source of authority that the Qing court needed but could not simply detach from the men commanding them.[1]

An army intended to strengthen the dynasty would help determine the terms on which it ended.

In Taipei, Shen Boyang is seeking the city’s highest office. The proposed Beiyang government already has a name. It still needs an election.

02A republic moves north

In February 1912, the Qing emperor abdicated. The following month, Yuan took office as provisional president in Beijing. The republic’s centre moved north from Nanjing.

The government based in Beijing from 1912 to 1928 came to be known as the Beiyang Government. Its history included constitutions, ministries, legislatures, appointments, and arguments over the distribution of authority. It also included the armed forces through which that authority could be secured or withdrawn.[3]

The provisional constitutional arrangement sought to constrain presidential power. Yuan subsequently dissolved the legislature and reorganised the government around a stronger presidency. Institutions remained, but their relations changed.[3]

A government can retain familiar offices while altering what those offices are able to do.

The proposed government also has a municipal address. Central News Agency reporting identifies Shen Boyang and Chiang Wan-an among the candidates in Taipei’s 2026 mayoral election.[4] A Beiyang government remains conditional.

A Chiang is already available.

03An additional title

Yuan did not stop at the presidency. In late 1915, he accepted a proposed imperial title. The Hongxian monarchy was intended to replace the republic with an empire under the same man. Opposition developed into the National Protection War, with Cai E and his allies raising resistance from Yunnan.

In March 1916, Yuan abandoned the monarchy. He died in June.[5]

The army, government, and personal authority through which Yuan had held the northern establishment together did not pass intact to a single successor. The presidency survived him; the position from which he had controlled it did not.

04Several centres within the north

After Yuan’s death, the Beiyang establishment divided.

The Anhui clique, associated with Duan Qirui, the Zhili clique, associated with figures including Feng Guozhang, Cao Kun, and Wu Peifu, and the Fengtian clique, associated with Zhang Zuolin, competed through alliances and war. Their geographical names made the divisions easy to place on a map. Their changing relations were less stable.

The Zhili–Anhui War of 1920 ended in the Anhui faction’s defeat. In 1922, Zhili and Fengtian fought each other. They fought again in 1924.[6]

These were not successive administrations taking turns within a settled system. Possession of Beijing mattered, but it did not dissolve the military and financial power held elsewhere.

The government remained in Beijing. Its occupants were less settled.

Across the northern districts, a recorded voice repeats: “小心奉系” — xiǎoxīn fèngxì. Beware the Fengtian clique.

It returns at regular intervals, attending to public safety and keeping history within earshot. Passengers have been advised.

05The revenue of years to come

The divisions also appeared in the accounts.

Taiwan’s Ministry of Finance historical account describes provinces withholding remittances after Yuan’s death. The central government depended heavily on remaining customs and salt revenues after debt obligations, while borrowing continued. Military authorities issued notes and collected land taxes in advance.[7]

Revenue could be separated from the year to which it belonged.

For the people required to pay, this was not an abstract rearrangement. A future harvest might already carry an obligation imposed in the present. For an incoming administration, the same practice could mean discovering that the resources attached to its office had been taken by its predecessors.

06An expedition from the south

In July 1926, the Northern Expedition began from Guangzhou. Chiang Kai-shek commanded the National Revolutionary Army, which advanced against the military powers standing between the southern Nationalist government and its project of national unification.[9]

The campaign entered a country divided among forces too substantial to be removed by civilian authority alone. Donald A. Jordan’s study places the expedition within a larger national revolution, involving political mobilisation as well as military advance.[10]

The northern armies held different territories and made different alliances. The expedition crossed their competing jurisdictions.

The northern establishment bore the name Beiyang. The commander advancing from the south bore the name Chiang.

Red, yellow, blue, white, and black ribbons cross in the air. In the early republican Five-Coloured Flag, the colours lay in horizontal bands. A surviving example in Taiwan’s National Museum of History preserves their order.[11]

The ribbons will not hold still for inspection.

07The journey out of Beijing

By 1928, the northern government’s position had become untenable.

Zhang Zuolin left Beijing for the northeast by train. On 4 June, a bomb planted by Japanese military personnel exploded as his train passed near Huanggutun, outside Mukden, now Shenyang. Zhang was fatally injured.

The assassination became known as the Huanggutun Incident.[12]

Between arrivals, the recorded voice repeats: “小心縫隙” — xiǎoxīn fèngxì. Mind the platform gap.

08A different flag

The end of the Beijing government and the settlement of the northeast did not occur on the same day.

Following his father’s death, Zhang Xueliang assumed power in the northeast. Negotiations continued through the second half of 1928. Records preserved by Academia Historica include communications urging an announcement of allegiance months before it occurred.[13]

On 29 December 1928, Zhang replaced the five-coloured flag with the Nationalist flag and announced acceptance of the Nanjing government’s authority. The event is known as the Northeast Flag Replacement, or Dongbei Yizhi.[14]

The flag changed. Regional commands and revenue arrangements remained beneath it, and Zhang retained substantial power in the northeast. The unification remained nominal in important respects.[14]

Below the flagpole, the five ribbons catch the wind.

09The platform remains

The candidates are still seeking votes. Passengers wait to board beneath the ribbons, while the recorded voice attends to the platform edge.

In December 1949, the Nationalist government withdrew to Taiwan, the island off the mainland’s southeastern coast.[15]

Goose Town’s accounts remain open. The horses have done their part. There is still a passenger list to settle before the next service arrives.

This time, heading southeast, whom should we take with us?
