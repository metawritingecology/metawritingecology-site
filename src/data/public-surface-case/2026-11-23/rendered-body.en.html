After the Votes Are Counted

A six-hour drive, a flashlight at a polling station, and what happens after a vote goes the other way.

Case date23 November 2026
Source cutoff23 November 2026

01

The road home

In June 2020, a man from Linyuan, a district of Kaohsiung, set out from Yilan. He picked up a former classmate in Taipei, and they drove south to vote in the recall of Kaohsiung’s mayor. The trip took six hours. He had driven home for the presidential election in January, too. He told a reporter he chose to drive so he could pick up friends along the way. [1]

The Taiwan Youth Association for Democracy helped 3,348 people travel home for the 2020 presidential election. Four years later, it organised 52 routes, including ferries to outlying islands. [2, 3] The routes made the trip cheaper. A seat on a bus still depended on getting time off, and a ticket home only helped if it got someone there before the polls closed.

On the day of the 2022 local elections, Taiwan Indigenous Television spoke to a man who had come back from Taoyuan to the South Link region. He returned two or three times a year. Election day was one of the occasions when he could expect to see friends and relatives at the same time. Other people in the same report talked about the strain of backing different candidates in a small community. [4]

Fire EX. played Southbound Night Bus (〈長途夜車〉) at a rally opposing the recall of legislator Chen Po-wei in 2021. After the song, singer Yang Ta-cheng urged people to get on a bus and go home to vote. [5] The appeal from the stage was for one side. The people waiting at home didn’t have to see the vote the same way.

02

Where the paths meet

In Summer Wars, four generations of the Jinnouchi family come to Ueda for their great-grandmother Sakae’s ninetieth birthday. When a crisis hits, Sakae calls people she knows. Others take on the threat online, and Wabisuke, who has walked out after a family quarrel, is called back to help. The birthday gets everyone to the same house. Once there is work to do, some of it has to happen somewhere else. [6]

In a recollection of the 1977 elections, photographer Chiu Wan-hsing describes going back to Guanyin, Taoyuan, to help his older brother watch the polls. They were there because they wanted the opposition candidate, Hsu Hsin-liang, to win. His brother brought a flashlight in case the lights went out during the count. [7] A supporter of another candidate could have wanted the same thing from that flashlight: to see each ballot as it was held up. How any one person marked a ballot was still private.

The family in Summer Wars can win together. At a polling station, people hoping for opposite outcomes can watch the same ballots being counted. For the July 2025 recalls, the Central Election Commission said that monitors recommended by both sides would be there, that members of the public could observe the count, and that each polling station would post its tally. [8] Someone could spend the morning helping people get to the polls and the evening watching their side lose. The tally posted at the station was there for them to read, too.

03

The next day

Kaohsiung voted to recall its mayor in 2020. In 2025, all 32 recalls put to a vote across two rounds failed: 31 for legislators and one for the mayor of Hsinchu. [9] Before the first round, pro-recall volunteers took shifts at Taoyuan Airport to welcome people flying back to vote. When the count came in, the pro-recall gathering outside the legislature ended early. [10, 11]

People who want to vote should have a way to do it without travelling home. The man who came back from Taoyuan would still have a reason to make the trip. His relatives and friends were there. They could see each other without anyone at the table having to say how they voted, or whether they voted at all.

For those of us who hoped the 2025 recalls would pass, the result wasn’t what we wanted. We accepted it. The disappointment could stay with election night. The next day, life went on.
