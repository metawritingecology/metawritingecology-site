How Taiwan Is Seen

A billboard in New York, a day off in Kaohsiung, and a sign at Taipei’s February 28 memorial.

Case date27 November 2026
Source cutoff27 November 2026

In September 2020, the pandemic forced Taiwanese groups in New York to cancel their annual march in support of Taiwan’s admission to the United Nations. A youth group called Keep Taiwan Free bought time on an electronic billboard in Times Square instead. It used the screen to tell passersby that Taiwan was donating masks overseas. Taiwan’s representative office in New York ran a separate campaign in the same area under the words “Taiwan Can Help.” The two campaigns came from different places, but both pointed to the same fact: Taiwan was contributing to the international pandemic response while remaining outside the UN system.

Six years later, Britain’s Channel 4 News framed a report on the Taipei mayoral election partly through semiconductors. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, better known as TSMC, makes advanced chips for companies around the world, and the growth of AI has drawn more attention to where those chips are made. The report also asked why Beijing was watching the race. Someone living in Taipei might start with a different question: what will change in the city after the election?

Turn over a power bank in a shop, then pick up a router. The same place might turn up in small print on both. A shopper who wasn’t looking for it can come across it twice in a minute. The label says where the object was made. It says very little about how people live there.

When BTS played Kaohsiung in November 2026, Jung Kook filmed parts of the city on a day off, including Pier-2 and Cijin. Two days earlier, bystanders had filmed members of the group at Liuhe Night Market. Both recordings showed Kaohsiung to people elsewhere. In one, a passerby decided when to take out a phone; in the other, the person people had come to see decided what to film and what to share.

Before that visit, the Japanese musician Fujii Kaze had already put a Kaohsiung breakfast shop, a park karaoke session and the Dragon and Tiger Pagodas on a concert screen. Long before either act came through, people in Kaohsiung were forming bands, running festivals and finding places to play. Those places are part of what a visitor gets to encounter on a day off. So is the room people leave when they recognise someone on the street and decide not to follow.

Taiwan has a familiar way of telling success stories, too. When someone wins an award abroad, a report may quickly add that the winner is Taiwanese. There is nothing wrong with taking pleasure in the news. But if the work drew little attention before the award, and the difficulties along the way never made the story, calling the winner “the pride of Taiwan” can be a way of borrowing a little of the credit. You can’t love someone only when they win. The same might be said of a place.

At Taipei’s February 28 memorial in 2026, the mayor apologised to the victims of the 1947 crackdown and their families. As he spoke, someone held up a sign: 「代誌無解決，原諒無可能。」 There can be no forgiveness while the matter remains unresolved. The Judicial Reform Foundation had used nearly the same words years earlier, in a different case. Chiang Kuo-ching had been exonerated and his family compensated. The foundation argued that the state still had to explain how the wrongful conviction happened and hold those responsible to account. These are distinct histories. In both, a public acknowledgement left questions that someone still had to ask.

The billboard in New York asked the world to look at what Taiwan had done. The sign in Taipei asked people in Taiwan to look at what was still unresolved. Both belong in the story. The people who stay to ask what went wrong have not given up on the place.
