From Formosa to Guandu

A name, an island, and people still converging

Case date15 November 2026
Original snapshot15 November 2026
Source cutoff15 November 2026

External public-discourse observation ≠ repository evidence. Date-bound research specimen; observation, structural reading and limits remain separate.

The gathering took place in Guandu on 15 November 2026, Taiwan time. This reading uses the linked event reports dated 14–15 November 2026 and the historical and institutional sources listed below. No precise hour-level event cutoff has been established.

On 15 November 2026, boats appeared on the Tamsui River.

Some people waited on the bank. Others arrived by road, by boat, or on stand-up paddleboards, moving toward Guandu. The temple, riverbank, bridge, and hills remained where they were. For a day, the people, flags, and ribbons changed how this landscape could be read. [1]

Supporters called it the “Battle of Guandu.” In Mandarin, the name echoes an ancient battle fought elsewhere. The echo did not turn this riverbank into an ancient battlefield. People had come to take part in an election here and now, rather than to reenact a war.

The ribbons were not all alike. Some referred to the candidate; others carried the words, patterns, or hopes of individual supporters. Gathering in one place did not mean that everyone had come for the same reason.

Movement 01

An island seen from elsewhere

Centuries ago, Formosa became one of the names through which Europeans came to know this island. Associated with beauty, the name also preserved a way of looking from outside.

Yet the island was not an empty place awaiting discovery. People had long lived between its mountains and sea, with languages, routes, and memories of their own. [2]

Taiwan lies between the ocean and the edge of a continent. Mountains run along its length, rivers descend toward the sea, and the Tropic of Cancer crosses its southern half. Monsoons and terrain produce different patterns of rain, wind, and seasons across the island. Geography gives Taiwan a distinctive outline while keeping it in relation with the world around it. [3]

Geography alone, however, does not determine how people live. Mountains and coastlines are visible. The restrictions within a political system are not always legible in a landscape.

Movement 02

When a name entered the street

Taiwan’s main island came under martial law in 1949. It was lifted in 1987. For thirty-eight years, assembly, speech, and political participation were restricted, and state power reached into many people’s everyday lives. [4]

In 1979, a magazine took the name Meilidao, usually rendered in English as Formosa. On 10 December that year, opposition activists held a Human Rights Day gathering in Kaohsiung. Suppression, arrests, and trials followed. These events became known as the Kaohsiung Incident, or the Formosa Incident. [5]

The name did not cause the event. But a word associated with beauty acquired another weight. It no longer referred only to an island viewed from the sea. It became part of the language through which people on that island claimed their rights.

The risks of entering the street then were not the same as those faced by people carrying ribbons to the river today. Placing the two scenes beside each other does not erase the distance between them. It makes that distance visible.

Movement 03

How change remains in everyday life

After martial law, institutional reform and social movements continued. In 1996, voters directly elected their president for the first time. In 2000, the presidency passed from one political party to another for the first time. [6]

Democracy was not a single work completed on a particular day. People, movements, parties, and institutions have taken part in its formation, and continue to make different demands of it.

Marriage equality, for example, emerged through years of civic advocacy, constitutional interpretation, and legislative debate. In 2019, same-sex couples gained the legal right to marry. Long-Term Care 2.0, introduced under a Democratic Progressive Party administration, expanded services on the foundations of the existing care system. These changes do not belong to one simple chain of causes. Each brought political decisions into daily life in a different way. [6]

Institutional change does not make everyone’s circumstances equal. Once a right enters the law, how people are treated at home, at school, on the street, and at work still requires attention.

Movement 04

Another harbour

In Kaohsiung, a waterfront long organised around port operations, industry, and transport has also been reconfigured over time.

Public spaces, exhibition facilities, and cultural venues have gradually taken shape in the Asia New Bay Area. Light rail has extended around the harbour and through the city, connecting places that once felt separate. The Kaohsiung Music Center, Pier-2, the waterfront, and other venues have become places to walk, travel, hear music, and gather. [7]

Large concerts also take place at the National Stadium, Kaohsiung Arena, and other locations. They are not the product of a single waterfront project. The city’s transformation involves planning across different periods, public infrastructure, industries, and the work of many people.

As a city becomes able to host more activities, the relations between transport, venues, and waterfronts also change how people imagine it.

Kaohsiung was the site of the Formosa Incident. Today, a passenger can enter Formosa Boulevard Station and travel to venues and neighbourhoods across the city. The station’s name carries history. The people passing through it need not be there to commemorate that history.

Both can be true.

Movement 05

Islands and currents

During this campaign, Puma Shen’s team presented its policies through an interactive online “HEART Policy Island.” Different themes became islands to explore, offering another point of entry alongside lists and policy papers. On 14 November, the “Ocean Currents Festival” brought some of these ideas into a physical, interactive setting. [8]

A policy island is a proposal for the future, not governance already delivered. It can make a set of policies easier to approach. Those policies still require discussion, challenge, and scrutiny.

The next day in Guandu, people arrived at the same place from the river and its banks. “Ocean currents” was a name used by supporters, and a way the gathering described itself.

A large crowd does not establish the merits of every policy. A joyful gathering cannot speak for an entire city. Yet boats and ribbons were there, on the water and along the bank, and people participated in ways they had chosen.

On the same island, a name can enter a map through a navigator’s hand, enter politics through a magazine, remain in a city as a station name, and travel to a riverbank with new participants.

Formosa, Meilidao, the policy islands, and the currents at Guandu are not different translations of one word. They belong to different moments and refer to different things.

Placed briefly together, they reveal something other than a history brought to a close: people on an island still finding names, forms, and ways to meet in public life.

Sources and scope
