Elections / Names / Public Record

The Name on the Ballot

A relay baton in Yunlin, a new name in Taoyuan, and what a campaign can make people say out loud.

Case date22 November 2026
Source cutoff22 November 2026

01

The baton

In August, at a press conference in Yunlin, the outgoing county magistrate handed a relay baton to her niece. The photographer could get both surnames into the caption with one word: Chang. Chang Li-shan was handing the baton to Chang Chia-chun, the Kuomintang’s nominee for county magistrate. The aunt also handed over a list of projects. The photograph[1] could suggest a handover. What had actually changed hands that day was a prop and a list. The office was still up for election.

Chang Chia-chun’s father, Chang Jung-wei, had also held the office. The name’s familiarity comes from more than the family tree. Chang Li-shan previously worked for Yunlin’s irrigation association, and her husband, Chang Yong-cheng, became chief executive of the National Farmers’ Association[2]. Those are institutional roles[3], not evidence that the family owns those institutions. But they help explain why the surname already means something in a county where farming and water are part of ordinary political life. In Taichung, the Yen family grew familiar through a different set of institutions: Yen Ching-piao held elected office and chaired Dajia’s Jenn Lann Temple[4]; his son Yen Kuan-heng won the by-election for the legislative seat his father had held[5].

Brazil offered a more direct version of the campaign gesture. Jair Bolsonaro chose his son Flávio as his party’s presidential candidate[6]. The father could help make him the nominee; he could not hand him the presidency. Flávio carried the name into the runoff, which Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won[7]. The Yunlin photograph leaves the same question open. A familiar name gives a candidate a head start in telling voters what comes next. How they vote is still open.

02

What the name field can hold

On November 19, a woman in a white period costume walked onto the stage of Taoyuan’s official city council candidates’ forum[8]. She kept Wang (王), her surname, and made fei (妃) the first character of her new given name. Together they form Wangfei, a royal title roughly meaning “the Princess.” The rest of her sixteen-character legal name reads like the premise of a time-travel story and names the person she is challenging. At the forum, she spoke against bullying and argued that lawsuits could discourage people from discussing a candidate’s conduct. That is her argument; the registration of her name is not a finding that the allegations are true.

There was a precedent in Chiayi. In 2022, a candidate surnamed Yen kept her surname and adopted a sixteen-character legal name. One part advocated designated zones for sex work. Another alluded to a case in Nantou involving allegations of sexual assault and minors. At the candidates’ forum[9], she linked the name change to her wish to keep the case in public discussion. She won a council seat, and the name survives in the official audio voter guide[10]. A line that usually just identifies the candidate now carries part of the reason she ran.

There is a limit to what that change can do. A legal name does not make its bearer immune from a lawsuit or remove the obligation to protect minors’ identities. In Britain in 2009, MP Paul Farrelly used a different kind of official record: he put a written question about the Trafigura injunction on Parliament’s agenda. The question could appear there even as The Guardian faced restrictions on reporting it. The parliamentary inquiry[11] drew a distinction between the protections of parliamentary proceedings and those available to a newspaper reporting them. The Taiwanese candidates had no such parliamentary privilege by virtue of registering. What they had was a place in an official record where their protest would ordinarily have had no field of its own.

03

Who gets to decide what the exchange is about?

There was an opening for another kind of family story in Taipei. On October 11, former legislative speaker You Si-kun posted a joke that paired DPP mayoral candidate Shen Po-yang’s adoption of a daughter with an insinuation about the ancestry of Shen’s opponent, incumbent mayor Chiang Wan-an. When reporters asked Shen about it[12], he called the remark humorous. He also said his opponent’s family was outside the scope of the discussion and that he wanted to focus on policy. The opening came from his own side. He chose not to use it.

Shen had spoken about adoption before, on his own terms. In a June interview[13], he described taking parenting classes during the adoption process. They had felt like another thing to get through, he said, until he ran into situations where the classes gave him something to draw on. In that interview he proposed a single point of contact for parenting advice and referrals[14]. The two occasions are worth keeping separate. One asked him to make use of somebody else’s family history. The other let him explain what his own experience had taught him about a service the city could provide.

Lula made a different choice when the remark was about him. On October 23, at Brazil’s final televised presidential debate, Flávio mocked him by referring to his nine fingers. Lula answered[15] by talking about the industrial accident in which he lost a finger, and demanded an apology. Flávio gave one. In an earlier debate[16], Lula had also answered accusations with a proposal: authorize access to protected records on both sides, including family records. The campaigns later submitted documents to the electoral court[17]; they did not publish all their bank accounts. In one exchange, he put work back into a story about his body. In the other, he asked what could actually be checked, and by whom.

In the Yunlin photograph, a baton changes hands and the family name stays the same. In Chiayi’s official audio guide, a name takes longer to read aloud. One uses a familiar name to tell a story about what comes next. The other puts a story that was hard to bring up into a line the election office already reads aloud. Taiwan has yet to count this November’s votes. Both records are already public.
